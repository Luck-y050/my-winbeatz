How the peg board works

The board forms a triangle of staggered pegs, with each row wider than the one above it. A ball released at the top has an even chance of moving left or right at every peg it touches. After crossing all rows, it lands in one of the bottom pockets, and there is always one more pocket than rows.

Because every bounce is a 50/50 turn, most balls settle near the middle and only a few reach the outer edges. Statisticians call this a Galton board, and the resulting shape is a binomial curve. In casino win beatz terms, common middle pockets pay less, while the rare edge pockets pay more.

Variance

Straight answers to five questions

Is every drop genuinely random?

On licensed sites, yes. Each bounce comes from an audited random number generator, and provably fair versions let you check later that the path was fixed before the ball moved.

Do more rows make the game pay better?

No. Extra rows add pockets and stretch the odds curve, so edge prizes become larger while their probability falls. The overall return stays about the same across board sizes.

Which risk level is the smart choice?

None is mathematically superior. Low risk keeps results smoother, while high risk puts more value into rare edge hits. The right choice depends on how much swing your bankroll can handle.
